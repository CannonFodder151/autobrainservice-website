Car Service Checklist Australia — Complete 2026 Guide

AutoBrain Team · 24 September 2026 · Maintenance

A proper car service checklist saves you money, keeps your warranty valid, and prevents breakdowns. Here's the complete checklist for Australian conditions — logbook intervals, brake jobs, tyres, seasonal prep, and a free printable version you can keep in the glovebox.

Why a checklist matters in Australia

Australian driving conditions — extreme heat, long distances, dust, stop-start city traffic — accelerate wear on every system. A checklist turns "I'll get to it" into "done." It also protects your warranty: the ACCC confirms you can use any qualified mechanic provided they follow the manufacturer's schedule and use appropriate parts.

The 9-step car service checklist

Run through these steps at every logbook interval (typically 6 months / 10,000 km). Tick each item, note the odometer, and log it in AutoBrain.

1. Check your logbook schedule

Open the manufacturer's logbook (or the digital version in AutoBrain) and confirm: next service due date, interval (time and km), and any major items scheduled at this interval — brake fluid, spark plugs, timing belt, coolant flush, transmission fluid. The logbook is your source of truth for warranty compliance.

2. Inspect fluid levels and condition

  • Engine oil: Level on dipstick, colour (amber = good, black = due, milky = coolant leak).
  • Coolant: Level in overflow tank, strength (hydrometer or refractometer — 50/50 mix for Australian climate).
  • Brake fluid: Should be clear to light amber. Dark fluid = moisture contamination; replace every 2 years.
  • Power steering: Level and condition (if hydraulic).
  • Transmission: Check via dipstick if equipped; otherwise note service interval.
  • Windscreen washer: Top up with quality additive.

3. Examine brakes — pads, rotors, fluid

Brake jobs are one of the most common and costly services. Here's what to check:

  • Pad thickness: View through wheel spokes. Minimum 3 mm; replace at 4–5 mm for safety margin.
  • Rotor condition: Look for scoring, heat cracks, or a raised lip at the outer edge.
  • Brake pedal feel: Firm and progressive. Spongy = air or old fluid. Low pedal = worn pads or leak.
  • Fluid age: Brake fluid is hygroscopic — absorbs water. Replace every 2 years regardless of km.

Cost reference: front brake pads $150–$350 per axle; rotors $200–$500 per axle. Full front brake job (pads + rotors) typically $400–$800 at an independent. See our car service cost guide for full pricing.

4. Check tyres — tread, pressure, age, alignment

Tyres are your only contact with the road. In Australia's heat, they degrade faster.

  • Tread depth: Legal minimum 1.5 mm; replace at 3 mm for wet safety. Use a tread gauge or the wear indicators in the grooves.
  • Pressure: Check cold, match the placard on the driver's door jamb (not the tyre sidewall). Include the spare.
  • Even wear: Uneven wear = alignment, suspension, or inflation issues. Rotate every 10,000 km.
  • Age: DOT date code on sidewall (e.g. "2322" = week 23, 2022). Replace at 5–6 years regardless of tread — UV and heat degrade the compound.
  • Damage: Cuts, bulges, sidewall cracks, embedded objects.

5. Test battery and charging system

Australian heat kills batteries faster than cold. Most auto parts stores (Supercheap, Repco, Battery World) will load-test free.

  • Terminals: Clean, tight, no corrosion (white/blue powder).
  • Load test: Voltage under cranking load should stay above 9.6 V.
  • Alternator output: 13.8–14.4 V at idle with lights and A/C on.
  • Start-stop batteries: AGM/EFB types need specialist testing; budget $150–$400 replacement.

6. Inspect belts, hoses and filters

  • Serpentine belt: Cracks, fraying, glazing, missing ribs. Replace every 80,000–100,000 km or 5–7 years.
  • Radiator hoses: Squeeze when cool — firm and springy, not soft, brittle, or swollen.
  • Engine air filter: Hold to light — if you can't see light through it, replace. Every 15,000–30,000 km.
  • Cabin air filter: Behind glovebox usually. Replace annually for allergy season and A/C efficiency.
  • Fuel filter: Check logbook interval (often 60,000–100,000 km).

7. Test all lights and wipers

Quick walk-around check — have someone operate lights while you watch, or use a reflective surface:

  • Headlights (low and high beam), park lights, indicators (front/rear/side), brake lights (including high-mount), reverse lights, fog lights, number plate light.
  • Wiper blades: replace annually in Australia — UV destroys rubber. Test for streaking, chatter, or missed spots.

9. Log everything and set reminders

The most important step: record it. AutoBrain's digital logbook captures date, odometer, cost, workshop, parts used, and photos of invoices. Set reminders for:

  • Next oil change (time or km based on oil type)
  • Brake inspection (every service)
  • Tyre rotation (every 10,000 km)
  • Major interval items (brake fluid, spark plugs, timing belt, coolant)

Dealer vs independent: checklist compliance

For warranty, the mechanic must follow the manufacturer's checklist exactly and use parts meeting the same specification. Independent mechanics can stamp your logbook — the ACCC confirms this. Keep invoices showing parts numbers and labour descriptions. AutoBrain stores photos of every invoice alongside the logbook entry.

Roadworthy certificate vs service checklist

Don't confuse a service checklist with a roadworthy certificate (RWC/pink slip/eSafety check). An RWC is a mandatory safety inspection for registration in NSW, VIC, QLD, ACT — it checks brakes, steering, suspension, lights, tyres, windscreen, seats, seatbelts. It does not cover engine oil, coolant, filters, or any fluid changes. A service checklist is preventive maintenance; an RWC is a legal minimum safety snapshot.

Frequently asked questions

What is included in a basic car service checklist in Australia?

A basic Australian car service checklist covers: oil and filter change, fluid level checks (coolant, brake, power steering, transmission), brake pad and rotor inspection, tyre tread and pressure check, battery test, belt and hose inspection, air and cabin filter check, all lights and wipers, and a road test.

How often should I run through a car service checklist?

Follow your manufacturer's logbook schedule — typically every 6 months or 10,000 km (whichever comes first) for a basic service. Run a quick DIY checklist (tyres, fluids, lights) monthly and before long trips. Major intervals at 40k, 60k, 100k km add brake fluid, spark plugs, timing belt, coolant flush.

Can I do my own car service checklist in Australia?

Yes, you can perform the inspection checklist yourself. For warranty compliance, the actual service work (oil change, brake fluid flush, etc.) must be done by a qualified mechanic who stamps your logbook. AutoBrain lets you log DIY checks separately from professional services.

What's the difference between a service checklist and a roadworthy certificate?

A service checklist is preventive maintenance you do regularly. A roadworthy certificate (RWC/pink slip) is a mandatory safety inspection for registration renewal or transfer in some states (e.g. NSW, VIC). RWC checks brakes, steering, suspension, lights, tyres, windscreen — it does not cover fluids, filters or engine health.
